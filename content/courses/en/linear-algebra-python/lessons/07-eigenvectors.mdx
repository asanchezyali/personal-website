When a transformation is applied to the plane, most vectors leave their original line: they change
direction and not merely length. Some, however, remain on it.

An **eigenvector** is a vector that, after the transformation, still lies on its own line. It may
have been stretched, contracted or reversed, but it has not rotated. The factor by which it has been
scaled is its **eigenvalue**.

$$A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$$

That equation is the complete definition: applying the matrix produces the same effect as
multiplying by a number.

## Identifying the eigen-directions

The dashed orange lines mark the eigen-directions. As the matrix is modified, it can be observed
that vectors on those lines are only scaled and never rotated.

Two configurations are particularly instructive:

- A **diagonal** matrix such as $\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$: the axes themselves are the
  eigen-directions, and the eigenvalues coincide with the diagonal entries. This is why diagonal
  matrices are straightforward to interpret: they are already expressed in their own basis.
- A **rotation** such as $\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$: no line remains invariant, since a
  rotation deflects every direction without exception. The figure will report that no real
  eigenvalues exist. Complex ones do exist, and they describe precisely the angle of rotation, but
  that development belongs to another lesson.

## The computation in NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 1],
              [1, 2]])

values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)

values       # array([3., 1.])
vectors      # the columns are the eigenvectors, normalised
# array([[ 0.707, -0.707],
#        [ 0.707,  0.707]])
```

The convention deserves attention: `np.linalg.eig` returns the eigenvectors **as columns** and not
as rows, a common source of error. Verification is immediate:

```python
v = vectors[:, 0]         # first eigenvector
A @ v                     # array([2.121, 2.121])
values[0] * v             # array([2.121, 2.121])  — they coincide
```

## Why eigenvectors matter

Eigenvectors answer the question of what a transformation does in essence. Setting aside the scaling
along its eigen-directions, it performs no other operation.

That property makes them a central tool in numerous contexts:

- **Principal component analysis** seeks the eigen-directions of the covariance matrix, that is, the
  axes along which the data exhibit the greatest variance. Reducing dimensionality consists in
  retaining the large eigenvalues and discarding the small ones.
- **PageRank** is the principal eigenvector of the web's link matrix.
- Applying $A$ repeatedly to an arbitrary vector aligns it with the eigen-direction of largest
  value, which is why Markov chains converge towards a stationary state.

In all these cases the question is the same: what persists when the transformation is repeated. And
the answer is always the dominant eigen-direction.

The course began with two operations, addition and multiplication by a scalar. From them arose
