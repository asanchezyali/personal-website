The question admits three different answers depending on who is asked, and all three are correct.

A **physicist** will describe a vector as an arrow in space, endowed with magnitude and direction,
which can be moved around without ceasing to be the same arrow. A **programmer** will define it as a
list of numbers. A **mathematician** will say it is an element of a vector space, and will
accompany the definition with a list of axioms.

All three characterisations are valid. For working purposes, however, the first supplies the
intuition and the second is what executes, and a large part of linear algebra consists precisely in
moving between the two.

## The vector as a displacement

It is useful to read $\vec{v} = (3, 2)$ not as a point but as a **displacement instruction**: three
units to the right and two upward, taking the origin as the starting position.

In NumPy that instruction is represented, quite literally, as a list:

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3, 2])
w = np.array([-2, 3])
```

## Addition as chaining

Here is the first idea worth establishing clearly. The sum of two vectors does not follow an
arbitrary rule about lists of numbers: it consists of performing one displacement and then the
other.

If $\vec{v}$ indicates "three right, two up" and $\vec{w}$ indicates "two left, three up", carrying
out both in sequence leads to $(1, 5)$, which is exactly the result of adding component by
component.

```python
v + w
# array([1, 5])
```

That correspondence — moving the starting point to the tip of the first arrow — is known as placing
the vectors *tip to tail*, and it explains why addition is defined in this way and not another.

## Multiplication by a scalar

Multiplying by a number **scales** the instruction without altering its direction:

```python
2 * v        # array([6, 4])   — twice the distance, same direction
-1 * v       # array([-3, -2]) — same line, opposite sense
0.5 * v      # array([1.5, 1.0])
```

A negative factor reverses the sense of the arrow. This is why numbers are called **scalars** in
this context: their sole function is to scale.

## Length

The magnitude of a vector follows from the Pythagorean theorem, since its components form the legs
of a right triangle:

$$\|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}$$

```python
np.linalg.norm(v)
# 3.605551275463989
```

A vector of length 1 is called a **unit vector**, and is useful for referring to a direction without
mixing it with magnitude:

```python
v / np.linalg.norm(v)
# array([0.83205029, 0.5547002])
```

## Why this matters later

Everything that follows rests on two operations: vector addition and multiplication by a scalar.
Nothing more. Matrices, determinants and eigenvectors are all built on those two.

A vector space is, in fact, *any* set in which both operations behave as they do here. That
generality explains why linear algebra appears in contexts bearing no apparent relation to geometry:
the *embeddings* of a language model are vectors because they admit addition and scaling, and that
condition alone is enough for the whole machinery to apply.
