A **vector space** over $\mathbb{R}$ is a set $V$ equipped with two operations, an addition
$V \times V \to V$ and a scalar multiplication $\mathbb{R} \times V \to V$, satisfying eight axioms:
associativity and commutativity of addition, existence of an identity element $\vec{0}$ and of
inverses, compatibility of scalar multiplication with multiplication of scalars, the identity
scalar $1$, and the two distributive laws. The elements of $V$ are called **vectors**.

The definition mentions neither arrows nor lists of numbers. Both are concrete realisations of the
same abstract object, which is why linear algebra applies in settings with no apparent geometric
content.

This lesson works in $V = \mathbb{R}^2$, where a vector is an ordered pair
$\vec{v} = (v_1, v_2)$ and the operations act component by component.

## Geometric realisation

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ the pair $\vec{v} = (3, 2)$ admits reading as a **displacement**: three units along
the first axis and two along the second. Drawing it as an arrow from the origin is a convention; what
defines the vector is the displacement, not its point of application.

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3, 2])
w = np.array([-2, 3])
```

## Addition

Addition is defined component by component:

$$
\vec{v} + \vec{w} = (v_1 + w_1,\; v_2 + w_2)
$$

Geometrically it corresponds to composing the two displacements: the second is applied from the tip
of the first. That construction is known as the parallelogram rule, or *tip-to-tail* placement, and
is not a convention but a direct consequence of the definition.

```python
v + w
# array([1, 5])
```

## Scalar multiplication

$$
\lambda\vec{v} = (\lambda v_1,\; \lambda v_2), \qquad \lambda \in \mathbb{R}
$$

The result remains on the line through the origin containing $\vec{v}$. Its magnitude is multiplied
by $|\lambda|$ and its sense reverses when $\lambda < 0$. That exclusive function is the origin of
the term **scalar**.

```python
2 * v        # array([6, 4])
-1 * v       # array([-3, -2])
0.5 * v      # array([1.5, 1.0])
```

## Norm

The **Euclidean norm** of $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is

$$
\lVert \vec{v} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}
$$

Its components are the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse measures
$\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$, so the expression coincides with the Pythagorean theorem.

```python
np.linalg.norm(v)
# 3.605551275463989
```

A vector of norm $1$ is called a **unit vector**. For $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$, the vector
$\vec{v}/\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$ is a unit vector and determines the direction of $\vec{v}$
independently of its magnitude.

```python
v / np.linalg.norm(v)
# array([0.83205029, 0.5547002])
```

## Scope of the definition

Everything that follows rests solely on the two operations of the definition. Linear combinations,
the span, linear transformations, the determinant and eigenvectors are all built on them without
requiring any additional structure.

Hence the generality of the result: any set whose elements admit addition and scalar multiplication
under the stated axioms inherits the entire theory. Polynomials of bounded degree, continuous
functions on an interval, and the *embeddings* of a language model are vector spaces for that
reason.
