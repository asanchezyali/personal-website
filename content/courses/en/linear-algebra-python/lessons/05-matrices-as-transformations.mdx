A map $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is a **linear transformation** if for all
$\vec{u},\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and all $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ it satisfies

$$
T(\vec{u} + \vec{v}) = T(\vec{u}) + T(\vec{v}), \qquad T(\lambda\vec{v}) = \lambda\, T(\vec{v})
$$

Those two conditions constitute the definition. From them it follows that $T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}$ and,
by induction, that $T$ commutes with any linear combination:

$$
T\!\left(\sum_i \lambda_i \vec{v}_i\right) = \sum_i \lambda_i\, T(\vec{v}_i)
$$

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ the geometric reading is that lines map to lines preserving uniform spacing, and
the origin stays fixed.

## Correspondence with matrices

Let $\{\hat{\imath},\hat{\jmath}\}$ be the canonical basis. Every $\vec{v} = (x,y)$ is written
$\vec{v} = x\hat{\imath} + y\hat{\jmath}$, and applying linearity

$$
T(\vec{v}) = x\,T(\hat{\imath}) + y\,T(\hat{\jmath})
$$

The transformation is therefore determined by the images of the basis vectors. That is the
information a matrix stores: **its columns are the images of the canonical basis**.

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T(\hat{\imath}) = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T(\hat{\jmath}) = \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}
$$

Substituting into the identity above yields

$$
A\vec{v} = x\begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{bmatrix}
$$

The matrix-vector product rule is not a convention: it is the only one compatible with linearity. The
correspondence between linear transformations $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $m \times n$
matrices is bijective.

| Matrix | Transformation |

| $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ | identity |
| $\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ | rotation by $\pi/2$ |
| $\begin{bmatrix}k&0\\0&k\end{bmatrix}$ | scaling by a factor $k$ |
| $\begin{bmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ | horizontal shear |
| proportional columns | projection onto a line |

The last case is degenerate: the image of $T$ is a subspace of dimension $1$ and the transformation
is not injective. The lesson on the determinant characterises it numerically.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0, -1],
              [1,  0]])     # rotation by π/2

v = np.array([3, 2])
A @ v                       # array([-2, 3])
```

The `@` operator implements the matrix product. The `*` operator performs the Hadamard product,
element by element, which corresponds to no composition of transformations.

The correspondence between columns and images of the basis is checked directly:

```python
A @ np.array([1, 0])    # array([0, 1])   = first column of A
A @ np.array([0, 1])    # array([-1, 0])  = second column of A
```
