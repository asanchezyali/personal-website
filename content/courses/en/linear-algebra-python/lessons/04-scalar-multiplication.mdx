Of the operations of linear algebra, multiplication by a scalar is the simplest to define and the
one that usually receives the least attention. Yet two of the most consequential decisions in
machine learning — how similarity between representations is compared, and how fast a model learns
— come down to choosing a number to multiply by.

## Definition

Given $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the product $\lambda A$ is
defined entry by entry:

$$
(\lambda A)_{ij} = \lambda\, a_{ij}
$$

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1., 2.],
              [3., 4.]])

2 * A
# array([[2., 4.],
#        [6., 8.]])
```

The expression `2 * A` does not traverse the matrix in Python. The scalar is treated as an array of
empty shape and broadcasting extends it over the $mn$ entries, which are processed in a compiled
loop.

## Properties

Multiplication by a scalar satisfies four identities which, together with those of addition, make up
the vector space axioms mentioned when $\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ was introduced:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda(A + B) &= \lambda A + \lambda B & \text{(distributive over matrix addition)}\\
(\lambda + \mu)A &= \lambda A + \mu A & \text{(distributive over scalar addition)}\\
\lambda(\mu A) &= (\lambda\mu)A & \text{(associative)}\\
1 \cdot A &= A & \text{(identity element)}
\end{aligned}
$$

```python
B = np.ones((2, 2))
lam, mu = 3.0, -0.5

np.allclose(lam * (A + B), lam * A + lam * B)    # True
np.allclose(lam * (mu * A), (lam * mu) * A)      # True
```

The scalar also commutes with transposition and moves freely between the factors of a matrix
product:

$$
(\lambda C)^\top = \lambda\, C^\top, \qquad \lambda(AB) = (\lambda A)B = A(\lambda B)
$$

```python
C = np.random.randn(2, 3)
np.allclose((lam * C).T, lam * C.T)              # True
```

That freedom of movement has a practical consequence: in a long expression, the scalar can be
applied wherever it is cheapest. Multiplying a $1000\times 1000$ matrix by $\lambda$ costs a million
operations; applying $\lambda$ to the resulting vector costs a thousand.

## The norm

The geometric effect of the scalar is stated in terms of the length of the vector. The **Euclidean
norm** of $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is

$$
\lVert \vec{v} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i^2}
$$

This is the notation that appeared in the previous lesson when bounding error propagation; here it
is defined. With it, the effect of the scalar is stated exactly:

$$
\lVert \lambda\vec{v} \rVert = |\lambda|\,\lVert \vec{v} \rVert
$$

```python
v = np.array([3., 4.])

np.linalg.norm(v)           # 5.0
np.linalg.norm(2 * v)       # 10.0
```

The absolute value is necessary: with $\lambda < 0$ the vector reverses its sense, but its length is
a non-negative quantity. Geometrically, $|\lambda| > 1$ dilates, $|\lambda| < 1$ contracts and
$\lambda < 0$ reflects through the origin. The direction, understood as the line the vector
generates, is unchanged in every case.

The Euclidean norm is not the only one in use. `np.linalg.norm` accepts the `ord` parameter:

| Norm | Definition | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_1$ | $\sum_i \lvert v_i \rvert$ | L1 regularisation, which induces sparsity |
| $\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_2$ | $\sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}$ | L2 regularisation, least squares |
| $\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_\infty$ | $\max_i \lvert v_i \rvert$ | bounds, gradient clipping |

All three satisfy $\lVert \lambda\vec{v} \rVert = |\lambda| \lVert \vec{v} \rVert$, a property known
as absolute homogeneity and required by the axiomatic definition of a norm.

## Normalisation

For $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$, choosing $\lambda = 1/\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$ produces a vector of unit
norm in the same direction:

$$
\hat{v} = \frac{\vec{v}}{\lVert \vec{v} \rVert}, \qquad \lVert \hat{v} \rVert = 1
$$

```python
u = v / np.linalg.norm(v)
np.linalg.norm(u)           # 1.0
```

The operation separates the information in a vector into two independent parts: the **direction**,
retained by $\hat{v}$, and the **magnitude**, left in the scalar $\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$. That
separation is the reason for its use in machine learning.

## Application: cosine similarity

When comparing two *embeddings*, what matters is usually the direction and not the magnitude. Two
texts on the same subject should count as close even if one is far longer than the other, and the
length of the vector tends to reflect that extent rather than the content.

**Cosine similarity** discards the magnitude by construction:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\lVert \vec{u} \rVert\,\lVert \vec{v} \rVert} = \hat{u} \cdot \hat{v}
$$

The $\lambda$ slider rescales $\vec{u}$. Its norm changes, its arrow lengthens or shortens, and the
value of $\cos\theta$ stays identical. The algebraic check is immediate:

$$
\frac{(\lambda\vec{u}) \cdot \vec{v}}{\lVert \lambda\vec{u} \rVert \lVert \vec{v} \rVert} = \frac{\lambda\,(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v})}{|\lambda|\,\lVert \vec{u} \rVert \lVert \vec{v} \rVert}
$$

For $\lambda > 0$ the factors cancel. That invariance under scaling is what makes cosine similarity
the standard measure in semantic retrieval.

```python
def cosine(u, v):
    return (u @ v) / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v))

cosine(v, 100 * v)     # 1.0: same direction, incomparable magnitudes
```

When the vectors are normalised in advance, similarity reduces to a dot product, and comparing one
vector against an entire corpus becomes a single matrix product:

```python
E = E / np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)   # unit rows
sims = E @ q                                        # similarity against the query q
```

That is the operation a vector database runs on every query.

## Application: the learning rate

Gradient descent is, in its basic form, multiplication by a scalar:

$$
\vec{w} \leftarrow \vec{w} - \alpha\,\nabla f(\vec{w})
$$

The gradient gives the direction of steepest increase; the scalar $\alpha$, the **learning rate**,
decides how far to move in the opposite direction. It is a single number, and whether training
converges depends on it.

With $f(\vec{w}) = \tfrac{1}{2}(w_1^2 + 4w_2^2)$, each step contracts coordinate $i$ by a factor
$1 - \alpha c_i$, where $c_i$ is the curvature along that axis. The method converges if and only if
$|1 - \alpha c_i| < 1$ for every coordinate, that is

$$
0 < \alpha < \frac{2}{\max_i c_i}
$$

In the figure that threshold is $0.5$. Three regimes are observable: with small $\alpha$ progress is
correct but slow; near the threshold the characteristic zigzag oscillation appears along the axis of
greatest curvature; above it, the iteration diverges.

```python
grad = np.array([0.5, -1.2])
w = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
lr = 0.1

w = w - lr * grad        # one step
```

The practical difficulty is that $\alpha$ is chosen once while the curvature varies by direction.
That disparity — the ratio of largest to smallest curvature, which is the condition number of the
Hessian — is what forces a small $\alpha$ and makes convergence slow. Adaptive methods such as Adam
replace the single scalar with a per-coordinate factor, and their whole advantage comes from that.
