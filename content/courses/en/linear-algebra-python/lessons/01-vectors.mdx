Ask three people what a vector is and you'll get three answers.

A **physicist** will say it's an arrow in space: it has magnitude and direction, and you can move it
around without it ceasing to be the same arrow. A **programmer** will say it's a list of numbers. A
**mathematician** will say it's an element of a vector space, and hand you a list of axioms.

All three are right. But for working, the first gives you intuition and the second is what runs —
and linear algebra is largely the craft of moving between them.

## A vector is a movement instruction

Think of $\vec{v} = (3, 2)$ not as a point but as an **instruction**: move 3 right and 2 up. The
origin is where you start.

In NumPy that instruction is literally a list:

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3, 2])
w = np.array([-2, 3])
```

## Adding is chaining instructions

Here's the first idea worth being clear about. Adding two vectors is **not** an arbitrary rule about
lists of numbers: it's doing one movement and then the other.

If $\vec{v}$ says "3 right, 2 up" and $\vec{w}$ says "2 left, 3 up", doing both in sequence leaves
you at $(1, 5)$ — which is exactly what adding component by component gives.

```python
v + w        # array([1, 5])
```

That correspondence — moving your starting point to the tip of the first arrow — is called putting
the vectors *tip to tail*, and it's why addition is defined this way and not some other.

## Scaling is stretching

Multiplying by a number **scales** the instruction without changing its direction:

```python
2 * v        # array([6, 4])   — twice as far, same heading
-1 * v       # array([-3, -2]) — same line, opposite way
0.5 * v      # array([1.5, 1.0])
```

A negative number flips the arrow. That's why numbers are called **scalars** here: their only job is
to scale.

## Length

A vector's magnitude comes from Pythagoras, because its components are precisely the legs of a right
triangle:

$$\|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}$$

```python
np.linalg.norm(v)    # 3.605551275463989
```

A vector of length 1 is called a **unit vector**, and it lets you talk about direction without
mixing in magnitude:

```python
v / np.linalg.norm(v)    # array([0.83205029, 0.5547002])
```

## Why this matters later

Everything that follows rests on two operations: **adding** vectors and **scaling** them. Nothing
else. Matrices, determinants, eigenvectors — all of it is built on those two.

A vector space is in fact *any* set where those two operations behave the way they do here. That's
why linear algebra turns up in places that don't look like geometry: a language model's embeddings
are vectors because they can be added and scaled, and that alone is enough for all this machinery
to apply.
