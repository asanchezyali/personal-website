We now have the two fundamental operations: addition and scaling. Combining them yields the central
construction of the entire course.

Given two vectors $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{w}$ and two numbers $s$ and $t$, the expression

$$s\vec{v} + t\vec{w}$$

is called a **linear combination**: each vector is scaled by its coefficient and the results are
added.

Once the values have been explored, the relevant question is this: **which points of the plane are
reachable?**

## The span

The set of all points reachable through linear combinations is called the **span** of $\vec{v}$ and
$\vec{w}$.

With the values above, the answer is *the entire plane*. Every point admits a pair $(s, t)$ that
reaches it. Two vectors pointing in different directions are enough to generate the whole of
$\mathbb{R}^2$.

```python
import numpy as np

v = np.array([2, 1])
w = np.array([-1, 2])

# Which combination reaches (4, 3)?
np.linalg.solve(np.column_stack([v, w]), np.array([4, 3]))
# array([2.2, 0.4])  →  2.2·v + 0.4·w = (4, 3)
```

That call to `solve` reflects what any resolution of a system of equations does at bottom:
determine the coefficients of a linear combination.

## Degeneration of the span

The significant case arises when both vectors point in the **same** direction.

With $\vec{v} = (2, 1)$ and $\vec{w} = (4, 2)$ — the second being twice the first — the result never
leaves a single line, whatever the coefficients. The span has gone from being a plane to being a
line.

In that situation the vectors are called **linearly dependent**: one of them contributes no new
information, being already contained in the other. When they are independent, the span covers the
entire plane.

```python
# The determinant detects exactly this condition (lesson 6)
np.linalg.det(np.column_stack([[2, 1], [4, 2]]))   # 0.0  → dependent
np.linalg.det(np.column_stack([[2, 1], [-1, 2]]))  # 5.0  → independent
```

## Basis

A set of independent vectors whose span covers the whole space constitutes a **basis**. The two
vectors used above form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

The customary reference basis is the one formed by $\hat{\imath} = (1, 0)$ and
$\hat{\jmath} = (0, 1)$:

An important observation follows from this: **writing a vector as $(3, 2)$ already involves using a
linear combination**, even though the basis is so habitual that it goes unmentioned. Coordinates are
always coordinates *with respect to some basis*.

That observation leads directly into the next module: if a change of basis modifies the coordinates,
then a matrix can be understood as the specification of where each basis vector goes.
