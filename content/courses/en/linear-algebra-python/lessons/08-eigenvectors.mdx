Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. A vector $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$ is
an **eigenvector** of $A$ if there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is the associated **eigenvalue**. The condition $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$ is part of
the definition: the zero vector satisfies the equality for every $\lambda$, and admitting it would
empty the notion of content.

Geometrically, $\vec{v}$ generates a line that $T_A$ leaves invariant: the image remains on that
line, scaled by $\lambda$ and with reversed sense when $\lambda < 0$.

## The characteristic equation

The definition is rewritten as $(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}$ with $\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}$, which
requires $A - \lambda I$ to be singular. By the criterion of the lesson on the determinant:

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0
$$

This is the **characteristic equation**. For $n = 2$ it is a polynomial of degree $2$ in $\lambda$,
so there are at most two real eigenvalues, and there may be none.

The dashed lines mark the eigen-directions. Two cases delimit the behaviour:

- $A = \operatorname{diag}(3,2)$: the coordinate axes are the eigen-directions and the eigenvalues
  are the diagonal entries. A diagonal matrix is expressed in a basis of eigenvectors, hence its
  simplicity.
- $A = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$: no line is left invariant, since a rotation by $\pi/2$
  deflects every direction. The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$, with no real roots.
  Over $\mathbb{C}$ the roots are $\pm i$ and encode the angle of rotation.

## Computation in NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 1],
              [1, 2]])

values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)

values       # array([3., 1.])
vectors
# array([[ 0.707, -0.707],
#        [ 0.707,  0.707]])
```

`np.linalg.eig` returns the eigenvectors **as columns** of the second output, normalised to unit
norm. Column $j$ corresponds to eigenvalue $j$:

```python
v = vectors[:, 0]
np.allclose(A @ v, values[0] * v)   # True
```

Every non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue, so the
returned result is a representative of a line, not a distinguished vector.

## Applications

Eigenvectors identify the invariant directions of a transformation, and therefore describe its
asymptotic behaviour under iteration.

- **Principal component analysis** diagonalises the covariance matrix. Its eigenvectors are the axes
  of maximum variance and its eigenvalues the variance explained by each. Dimensionality reduction
  consists in retaining those of largest eigenvalue.
- **PageRank** is the eigenvector associated with eigenvalue $1$ of the transition matrix of the link
  graph.
- In a **Markov chain** with transition matrix $P$, the stationary distribution $\pi$ satisfies
  $\pi P = \pi$, that is, it is a left eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$. Convergence towards it
  depends on the second largest eigenvalue in modulus.

The common pattern is iteration: $A^k\vec{u}$ aligns with the eigen-direction of largest modulus as
$k$ grows, provided that eigenvalue is unique in modulus.

## Recapitulation

The course starts from two operations, addition and scalar multiplication, which define a vector
space. From them follow linear combinations and the span. Linear transformations are the maps
compatible with both operations, and are determined by the image of a basis, which establishes the
correspondence with matrices. The determinant measures the signed area scale factor and
characterises singularity. Eigenvectors identify the invariant directions.
