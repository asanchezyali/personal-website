Apply a transformation to the plane and almost every vector gets knocked off its original line: it
changes direction, not just length.

Almost every one. Some don't.

An **eigenvector** is a vector that, after the transformation, still lies on its own line. It may
have stretched, shrunk or flipped, but it didn't rotate. The factor it scaled by is its
**eigenvalue**.

$$A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$$

That equation is the whole definition: applying the matrix does the same thing as multiplying by a
number.

## Find them

The dashed orange lines are the eigen-directions. Move the matrix and watch: on those lines vectors
only stretch, never turn.

Try two cases:

- A **diagonal** matrix like $\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$: the axes themselves are the
  eigen-directions, and the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries. That's why diagonal matrices are
  easy — they're already written in their own basis.
- A **rotation** like $\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$: no line survives, because a rotation
  twists absolutely everything. The widget will tell you there are no real eigenvalues. There are
  complex ones, and they describe precisely the angle of rotation — but that's another lesson.

## In NumPy

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 1],
              [1, 2]])

values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)

values       # array([3., 1.])
vectors      # the columns are the eigenvectors, normalised
# array([[ 0.707, -0.707],
#        [ 0.707,  0.707]])
```

Mind the convention: `np.linalg.eig` returns eigenvectors **as columns**, not rows. It's a classic
mistake. Always check:

```python
v = vectors[:, 0]         # first eigenvector
A @ v                     # array([2.121, 2.121])
values[0] * v             # array([2.121, 2.121])  — same
```

## Why they matter

Eigenvectors answer the question *"what does this transformation essentially do?"*. Discounting the
stretching along its eigen-directions, it does nothing else.

That makes them the central tool in a lot of places:

- **PCA** looks for the eigen-directions of the covariance matrix: the axes along which the data
  varies most. Reducing dimensions is keeping the large eigenvalues and discarding the small ones.
- **PageRank** is the principal eigenvector of the web's link matrix.
- Applying $A$ many times to an arbitrary vector aligns it with the dominant eigen-direction —
  which is why Markov chains converge to a steady state.

In all of those the question is the same: what survives repeating the transformation. And the answer
is always the dominant eigen-direction.

## Where we got to

We started with arrows and two operations, adding and scaling. Out of those came linear
combinations, and out of those the span. Matrices turned out to be the functions that respect those
two operations, their columns storing the destination of the basis. The determinant measured how
much area stretches and flagged when a transformation is irreversible. And eigenvectors revealed
what a transformation does, with the noise removed.

None of it needed a memorised rule.
