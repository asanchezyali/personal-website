The determinant is usually introduced as a formula to memorise: $ad - bc$. But it's a number with a
sharp geometric meaning, and once you see it the formula stops being necessary.

## What it measures

The determinant of a matrix is **the factor by which area is multiplied** when you apply the
transformation.

Take the unit square — the one spanned by $\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$, of area 1. Apply the
matrix. The area of the resulting figure *is* the determinant.

The purple region is the image of the unit square. Move it and watch the number follow.

- $\det = 1$ → area unchanged. Rotations and shears are like this.
- $\det = 3$ → everything takes up three times the room.
- $\det = 0.5$ → everything shrinks by half.

## Zero means collapse

Drive the determinant to zero. The purple region disappears, because the entire plane flattened onto
a line.

This ties the three previous lessons into one sentence: **$\det A = 0$ if and only if the columns of
$A$ are linearly dependent**, if and only if the span collapses, if and only if $A$ has no inverse.
Four ways of saying the same thing.

```python
import numpy as np

np.linalg.det(np.array([[2, 1], [1, 1]]))   # 1.0  → invertible
np.linalg.det(np.array([[2, 4], [1, 2]]))   # 0.0  → singular
```

## The sign

Now something the formula doesn't suggest: the determinant can be **negative**.

Set $a = -1$ and leave the rest at the identity. The determinant becomes $-1$. The area is still 1
in magnitude, but the plane has been **flipped**: what used to be on the left of $\hat{\imath}$ is
now on its right.

The sign records orientation. Positive, the plane keeps its handedness; negative, it was reflected.

## Why that formula

With the interpretation in hand, $ad - bc$ stops being arbitrary.

The columns of the matrix are the two sides of the parallelogram the unit square became: $(a, c)$ and
$(b, d)$. The area of a parallelogram with those sides is exactly $|ad - bc|$ — it drops out of
subtracting the leftover triangles from the enclosing rectangle. The formula *is* the area.

## A property that comes free

$$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$$

Which in words is almost obvious: if $B$ triples area and $A$ doubles it, doing both multiplies it by
six. Proving it with the formulas is tedious; seeing it geometrically is immediate.

```python
A = np.array([[2, 0], [0, 1]])
B = np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]])

np.linalg.det(A @ B)                          # 2.0
np.linalg.det(A) * np.linalg.det(B)           # 2.0
```
