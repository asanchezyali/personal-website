Popular accounts of language models tend to stop at the metaphor: the model "predicts the next
word", "has read the whole internet", "learns like a child". None of those sentences answers the
questions that matter when building a system. How much text is required for a given compute budget.
Why a model that fills in blanks cannot generate. What is actually being optimised when a loss is
minimised over billions of tokens.

This course develops those answers from the original formulations. Each lesson starts from an
explicitly written objective, derives it where the derivation is short, and checks its quantitative
claims against the published figures of real models. The interactive figures allow the parameters of
each expression to be manipulated before its analysis is read.

The **first module** treats pre-training as a paradigm: why self-supervision displaced per-task
supervised learning, what the scaling laws promise and where their first formulation was wrong, and
the two ways of adapting an already trained model.

The series continues with the objective of each architecture family: the causal decoder and its
equivalence with maximum likelihood, the bidirectional encoder and masked language modelling, and
the encoder-decoder variants.

The contents follow Xiao and Zhu, *Foundations of Large Language Models*
([arXiv:2501.09223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09223)), with primary references cited in each lesson.
