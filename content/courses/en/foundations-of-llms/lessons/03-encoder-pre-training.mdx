The previous lesson described training a decoder, where the target comes for free: the next token is
already in the sequence. An encoder has no such convenience. It reads the whole sequence and returns
one vector per position, and a vector of real numbers admits no comparison against any reference.
This lesson is about how it is supervised instead, what is kept when training ends, and why the
family's most obvious auxiliary objective turned out to measure something other than intended.

## The problem of supervising an encoder

The encoder's output is a matrix of representations:

$$
\mathbf{H} = \text{Encode}_\theta(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \mathbf{H} = (\mathbf{h}_0, \dots, \mathbf{h}_m)
$$

There is no correct $\mathbf{H}$ to compare it against. The solution is to add, temporarily, a head
turning each vector into a distribution over the vocabulary, which can be compared to a reference:

$$
\mathbf{p}_i = \text{Softmax}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{h}_i)
$$

Both $\theta$ and $\mathbf{W}$ are trained jointly, and when training ends **$\mathbf{W}$ is
discarded**. The head is scaffolding: it exists only to give the error somewhere to enter through.
What is kept is the encoder.

The asymmetry with the decoder is worth naming, because it explains the rest of the lesson. There
the output head is kept, because generating is the point. Here it is thrown away, because the point
was never to reconstruct hidden words: it was to obtain the representations produced along the way.

## What is kept is the contextual representation

The product of pre-training is one vector per token that depends on the sentence it appears in. That
is the difference from a static representation of the word2vec kind, where every vocabulary entry
has a fixed vector. A polysemous word then forces a compromise: the vector for *bank* must sit
between the financial sense and the river's edge, and represents neither well.

A bidirectional encoder has no such constraint. In "he sat on the river bank" and "the bank refused
him the loan", the token *bank* goes in identical and comes out different, because the two
representations are built by attending to different contexts. The representation stops being a
property of the word and becomes a property of the occurrence.

The idea predates BERT. Peters and co-authors introduced it in 2018 with ELMo, using a bidirectional
recurrent network; the contribution of the attention-based encoder was to make those representations
considerably better and, above all, easy to fine-tune.

This is why the fine-tuning of the introductory lesson is cheap. The network added on top does not
learn to understand language: it learns to **read** representations that already distinguish each
word's senses from its context.

## The two cracks in masking

Masked language modelling was formulated in the first lesson. Yang and co-authors raised two
objections to it in 2019 that are worth stating precisely, because they motivate everything that
follows.

The first is the **pretrain-finetune discrepancy**. The `[MASK]` symbol appears during pre-training
and in no text afterwards. The first lesson described the 80/10/10 split with which BERT softens the
problem, but its nature should be clear: it is a mitigation, not a solution. The mismatch is still
there.

The second is deeper and usually overlooked. In predicting several hidden positions at once, the
objective treats them as **conditionally independent** given the visible context:

$$
P(x_{\mathcal{M}} \mid \mathbf{x}_{\setminus\mathcal{M}}) \approx \prod_{i \in \mathcal{M}} P(x_i \mid \mathbf{x}_{\setminus\mathcal{M}})
$$

In the sentence "New [MASK] is a [MASK]", reconstructing *York* evidently depends on having
reconstructed *New*, and the objective cannot represent that dependency: each blank is predicted
without knowing what went into the others. The causal decoder does not suffer from this, because its
chain factorisation is exact and approximates nothing.

## Permuted language modelling

XLNet proposed keeping bidirectionality without resorting to masking. The idea is to leave the
positions untouched and **randomise the order in which tokens are predicted**, predicting each one
autoregressively from those already predicted.

The figure shows the essential point. Each token keeps its position in the sentence, but the target
of a step may condition on tokens lying **to its right**, as long as they come earlier in the
factorisation order. Context on both sides is thus recovered without introducing any artificial
symbol, and without the independence assumption: every prediction sees the previous ones.

Two details make the construction workable. The first is **partial prediction**: only the last part
of each order is predicted and the rest serves as context, because predicting from an empty prefix
converges extremely slowly. The second is **two-stream attention**, and the reason it exists is
worth explaining. To predict position $z_t$ the model needs to know *which position* it is, but not
*which token* occupies it; conventional attention produces a representation that already incorporates
that position's content, so it would leak the answer. XLNet therefore keeps two streams: a content
stream, which includes the token, and a query stream, which knows the position but not its content
and is the one that makes the prediction.

## The classification objectives, and one that failed

Masking is not the only self-supervision available. **Classification** tasks whose label follows from
document structure can also be invented from raw text.

BERT included one: **next sentence prediction**. Given two sentences, decide whether the second
follows the first. The positive examples are real consecutive pairs and the negatives are formed by
taking the second sentence from a random document.

The objective looks reasonable and is not. Since the negative comes from a different document, it
almost always concerns **another topic**, and detecting a change of topic is far easier than judging
coherence between two sentences. The task admits a shortcut, and the model takes it.

The evidence is quantitative and comes from ALBERT. A model trained on next sentence prediction
solves the **sentence ordering** task —where the negative is the same two sentences swapped, with
identical vocabulary and topic— with $52.0\%$ accuracy, indistinguishable from chance. Conversely, a
model trained on ordering solves the original task with $78.9\%$. The asymmetry proves the first
never measured coherence: it measured topic shift, which is precisely what masking already taught.

The practical conclusion arrived by two routes. RoBERTa removed the objective with no loss, and
ALBERT replaced it with ordering, with consistent gains on tasks spanning several sentences. A
self-supervised objective can be plausible, cheap and still measure something other than intended;
checking requires designing the negative with care.

## ELECTRA: signal at every position

The introductory lesson noted that masking produces gradient at only 15% of positions. ELECTRA
starts from that observation. A small **generator**, trained by ordinary masking, replaces some
tokens with plausible alternatives. A **discriminator** —the model that is kept— then classifies
**every** position of the sequence as original or replaced.

The difference is sample efficiency: supervision reaches all tokens instead of a seventh of them.
The published results are notable. ELECTRA trained for four days on a single GPU outperforms GPT on
the GLUE benchmark, despite GPT using **thirty times more compute**; at scale it matches RoBERTa and
XLNet with **less than a quarter** of their compute, and outperforms them at equal budget.

One clarification the setup invites confusion about: **it is not a generative adversarial network**.
The generator is trained on its own masking objective, not against the discriminator. There is no
adversarial game, only a source of difficult substitutions.

## Encoders did not disappear

The usual account ends here, with decoders displacing encoders, and it is misleading. Adaptation
through the context proved superior for general use, as the introductory lesson describes, but
encoders hold the ground they were designed for: producing representations. Semantic search, result
reranking and large-scale classification still rest on models of this family, because a dense
representation of a sentence is computed once and reused indefinitely, whereas a generated answer
has to be produced every time.

The cost difference is structural and remains the one the mask imposed: the encoder traverses the
sequence once; the decoder, one token at a time.

## The next step

Decoders generate and encoders represent. The open question is whether one model can do both. The
answer is the encoder-decoder family, which the next lesson develops: T5's text-to-text
pre-training, prefix language modelling, and the repertoire of corruptions with which BART learns to
reconstruct an altered document.
