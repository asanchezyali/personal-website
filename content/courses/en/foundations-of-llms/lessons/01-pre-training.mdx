For most of the history of natural language processing, every task received its own model, trained
from scratch on its own labelled dataset. Sentiment analysis, translation and question answering
were separate problems, with separate annotations and separate effort. The cost of annotation set a
ceiling: no task could have more data than someone had labelled by hand for it.

Pre-training inverts the order. A large model is first trained on **unlabelled** text, through an
objective the text itself supplies, and only afterwards adapted to specific tasks. This lesson
examines what that inversion gains, what it costs, and what its two real limits are.

## The model as a function

The notation is worth fixing before discussing training. Every model this course treats is a
function of a token sequence:

$$
\mathbf{o} = g(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_m;\, \theta) = g_\theta(x_0, \dots, x_m)
$$

where $x_0$ is a conventional start symbol —`[CLS]` or `<s>`—, $\theta$ are the parameters and
$\mathbf{o}$ is the output. What $\mathbf{o}$ is depends on the use: a distribution over the
vocabulary if the model predicts tokens, or a vector representation of the sequence if the model
encodes it. Pre-training consists of estimating $\theta$ once, on a general corpus, so that
$g_{\hat\theta}$ can be reused across many problems.

A model trained on broad data at scale and subsequently adapted to diverse tasks is what Bommasani
and co-authors named a **foundation model**. The term describes an economic relation rather than an
architecture: the cost moves from *per task* to *once*.

## Three ways to pre-train

The word pre-training predates deep learning and denotes three distinct procedures, differing in
what data the first stage consumes.

**Unsupervised pre-training** optimises a criterion unrelated to any task —reconstructing the input,
for instance— and then trains on labelled data. It supplies a better starting point and acts as a
regulariser.

**Supervised pre-training** trains on one labelled task and transfers to another. It is the recipe
computer vision popularised with ImageNet, and it presupposes that the two tasks are related.

**Self-supervised pre-training** builds the labels from the text itself. Predicting a hidden word
produces a supervised objective whose label is the word that was there all along; no annotator is
involved.

The decisive difference is one of availability. The first two procedures need labels at some point,
and therefore inherit the very ceiling that was to be removed. The third needs none, and turns any
text into training signal. That is why corpora went from millions to trillions of tokens.

## What the scaling laws promise

Removing the label ceiling is only useful if adding data and compute keeps improving the model.
Kaplan and co-authors established in 2020 that the loss of a language model decreases as a **power
law** in three quantities: the number of parameters $N$, the volume of data $D$ and the compute
spent $C$.

$$
L(N) \propto N^{-\alpha_N}, \qquad L(D) \propto D^{-\alpha_D}, \qquad L(C) \propto C^{-\alpha_C}
$$

The exponents they fitted are small: $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$, $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$ and
$\alpha_C \approx 0.050$. That magnitude deserves a pause. With $\alpha_C = 0.050$, multiplying the
compute budget by ten reduces the loss by a factor $10^{-0.050} = 0.891$, that is, by roughly
**11%**. Multiplying it by a hundred reduces it by 21%.

The power law therefore promises no spectacular gain per order of magnitude. It promises something
different and more valuable to whoever is planning: **regularity**. The performance of the next
order of magnitude can be estimated before it is spent. That is what turned scaling into an
engineering decision rather than a gamble.

## Where the first formulation was wrong

Kaplan's laws describe how the loss improves as each factor is raised separately. The question a
real budget poses is a different one: given a fixed compute $C$, how should it be split between
parameters and data? Kaplan and co-authors concluded that model size should be prioritised. Hoffmann
and co-authors showed in 2022 that this conclusion was incorrect.

The starting point is the standard estimate of the cost of training a dense transformer:

$$
C \approx 6ND
$$

The factor $6$ collects the operations per token and per parameter in the forward and backward
passes. With $C$ fixed, the expression defines a family of possible splits: large models on little
data, or small models on a great deal. Hoffmann's result is that the optimum scales **both factors
equally**, which within their studied range amounts approximately to

$$
D \approx 20N
$$

Combining the two expressions gives $C \approx 120N^2$, from which the optimal split for a given
budget is determined:

$$
N_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{\frac{C}{120}}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} = 20\,N_{\text{opt}}
$$

The check is that the formula reproduces the authors' own decisions. Chinchilla was trained with
$C \approx 5.76\times10^{23}$ operations, and the expression above returns
$N_{\text{opt}} \approx 69.3$ billion parameters and $D_{\text{opt}} \approx 1.39$ trillion tokens:
the published figures are 70 billion and 1.4 trillion.

Applied to GPT-3 the conclusion is uncomfortable. With 175 billion parameters and 300 billion
tokens, its budget was $C \approx 3.15\times10^{23}$; the optimal split for that same budget would
have been a model of some 51 billion parameters trained on 1.02 trillion tokens. That is, a model
**three times smaller** fed **three times more text**. Gopher, with 280 billion parameters on the
same 300 billion tokens, fell further still from the frontier, and Chinchilla —at 70 billion—
outperformed it on virtually every evaluation at comparable compute.

The methodological lesson outlives the case. An empirical law fitted within a range describes that
range; the operational conclusion drawn from it depends on what was held fixed while fitting. Kaplan
and co-authors used a single learning-rate schedule across all model sizes, which systematically
penalises models trained for longer and biases the optimum towards large models.

## The two self-supervised objectives

What objective is optimised remains to be specified. The two main answers give rise to the two
families of models.

**Masked language modelling**, introduced with BERT, hides around 15% of the tokens and trains the
model to reconstruct them from context on both sides:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log P_\theta\!\left(x_i \mid \mathbf{x}_{\setminus\mathcal{M}}\right)
$$

where $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of hidden positions. The actual procedure carries a detail usually
omitted, and it is not cosmetic: of that 15%, only 80% is replaced by `[MASK]`, while 10% is
replaced by a random token and the remaining 10% is left untouched. The reason is that `[MASK]`
appears in no real text, so a model trained solely on that marker would condition its
representations on a symbol it will never see again. The mixture forces a useful representation at
every position, not only where a marker sits.

**Causal language modelling** predicts each token from the preceding ones, which by the chain rule
factorises the probability of the whole sequence:

$$
P_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{t=1}^{m} P_\theta\!\left(x_t \mid x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\right)
$$

Taking logarithms, maximising this probability over a corpus is exactly estimating $\theta$ by
maximum likelihood. There is no auxiliary objective: the model fits the distribution of the observed
language.

Two consequences follow from the figure, and they explain the recent history of the field.

The first is that a bidirectional model **cannot generate** left to right. If every position sees
the complete sequence, predicting position $i$ amounts to reading it: the answer is part of the
input. That is why masking is necessary in BERT, and why its objective is defined only over the
hidden positions. A causal model has no such problem, because the triangular mask guarantees that
position $i$ never observes its own target.

The second is a difference in efficiency that the figure quantifies. With $n$ positions, the causal
mask leaves $n(n+1)/2$ pairs visible and produces training signal at **every** position of each
sequence. Masking contributes gradient at only 15% of the positions, so each sequence yields roughly
one seventh as much. Clark and co-authors made this point when introducing ELECTRA, and it is one of
the reasons the causal family proved more favourable to scaling, independently of the quality of its
representations.

## Adaptation by fine-tuning

A pre-trained encoder turns a sequence into vectors:

$$
\mathbf{H} = \text{Encode}_{\hat\theta}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \mathbf{H} = (\mathbf{h}_0, \mathbf{h}_1, \dots, \mathbf{h}_m)
$$

It does not constitute a system on its own. To classify, a head is added and the composed model is
trained on a labelled set:

$$
(\tilde\omega, \tilde\theta) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\omega,\theta} \sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)} \text{Loss}\!\left(F_{\omega,\theta}(\mathbf{x}),\, y\right)
$$

Since the starting point is not random, the labelled set can be small and the run is cheap. The
result, however, is one model per task: $\tilde\theta$ is no longer good for anything else.

## Adaptation through the context

A causal model good enough at continuing text admits an alternative. If the task is recast as a text
the model must complete, it is solved **without modifying any parameter**.

```text
Polarity ∈ {positive, negative, neutral}.

Input: The weather here is wonderful.
Polarity: positive

Input: I love the food at this place.
Polarity:
```

Including worked examples inside the context itself lets the model infer the pattern with no weight
update. The phenomenon is called **in-context learning**, and the examples, demonstrations. With
demonstrations one speaks of the few-shot regime; with only an instruction, of the zero-shot regime.

The contrast with fine-tuning is one of kind, not of degree. Fine-tuning produces one model per
task; the context produces one model that serves them all. That difference explains much of the
field's shift.

## The shift

The two forms of adaptation correspond to two eras. BERT-style encoders, trained by masking and
adapted by fine-tuning, dominated between 2018 and 2020. GPT-style decoders, trained by next-token
prediction and adapted through the context, hold that place today. The foundation is the same
—self-supervised pre-training at scale— and what changed was the adaptation mechanism, which was in
turn determined by the mask.
