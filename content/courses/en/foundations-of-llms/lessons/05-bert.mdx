The previous module developed the self-supervised objectives in the abstract. BERT is the concrete
instance that popularised them, and about its objectives almost everything has been said: masking and
its 80/10/10 split were covered in the introductory lesson, and next sentence prediction, along with
the evidence that it measured something else, in the lesson on the encoder.

This lesson covers what those left out: how the two objectives combine, what the input is actually
made of, where the parameters go, and a design decision that turned out to matter as much as the
proportion of hidden tokens.

## Two objectives summed

BERT is pre-trained by optimising **both** tasks at once, and the combination is the simplest
possible:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BERT}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{NSP}}
$$

There is no coefficient weighting one against the other, and no schedule alternating between them.
Every batch produces both losses and the gradient is that of the sum.

The division of labour is deliberate. Masking operates **within** the sentence, on the relation
between a word and its immediate context. Next sentence prediction operates **between** sentences, on
the relation between two segments. Downstream tasks need both scales: sentiment analysis acts on one
sentence, textual inference on a pair.

The outcome is worth recalling, already treated in the lesson on the encoder: the second task did not
survive scrutiny. RoBERTa removed it with no loss and ALBERT showed it measured topic shift rather
than coherence. The sum above describes the model as published, not the recipe one would recommend
today.

## The input representation

Before the first layer, each token becomes a vector that is the **sum of three** lookups into three
different tables.

The first is the **token** table, with one entry per vocabulary element. BERT uses WordPiece, so the
vocabulary holds subwords rather than words: $30{,}522$ entries in the lowercase model.

The second is the **position** table. Unlike the original transformer, which used fixed sinusoidal
functions, BERT **learns** a table of positions, with one row per index up to the maximum allowed.
That is where the $512$-token context limit comes from: it is not a restriction of the attention
mechanism but the number of rows in that table.

The third is the **segment** table, with only two rows, marking whether a token belongs to the first
segment or the second. It is what allows a pair of sentences to be packed into a single sequence.

Two markers are added to that sequence. `[SEP]` separates the segments and closes the input. `[CLS]`
occupies position zero, and its output vector is used as a summary of the whole pair, which is what
the second task's classifier reads. It is worth noting that `[CLS]` summarises nothing by
construction: it is an ordinary position whose representation takes on that role **because training
forces it** to.

## The parameter budget

The figures usually quoted —110 million parameters in the base version, 340 in the large one— follow
exactly from the published configuration, and the breakdown is more informative than the total.

With twelve layers, hidden dimension $768$ and an inner dense network of $3072$, each layer
contributes some $7.09$ million parameters, and the twelve sum to $85.05$ million. Adding the
embedding tables and the pooler, the total comes to $109.5$ million, which is the published figure.

The datum deserving attention is another. In the base version the token embedding table takes
$23.44$ million parameters, that is, **21% of the model**. And that table computes nothing: it is a
lookup, one row per vocabulary entry, identical whatever the context. A fifth of the model is
dedicated to storing static vectors.

One of ALBERT's decisions follows from this: it factorises the table into two matrices, $V \times E$
and $E \times H$ with $E$ much smaller than $H$. With $E = 128$ the cost falls from $23.44$ to $4.01$
million, nearly six times less, without touching the capacity of the layers. In the large version the
proportion drops to 9%, because the layers grow faster than the vocabulary: the problem is specific
to small models.

## The granularity of masking

The introductory lesson covered **how many** tokens are hidden and **what** they are replaced with. A
third decision remains, which BERT settled in the simplest way and its successors corrected: **which
unit** is hidden.

BERT masks independent subwords. Since the vocabulary is of subwords, a long word is split into
several pieces, and hiding only one of them leaves the others in plain sight.

The problem is visible in the figure. Faced with `pre [MASK] ##ed`, reconstructing the middle piece
requires no understanding of the sentence: it is enough to know which subwords fit between that
prefix and that suffix. The task degenerates into an exercise in morphology solvable locally, and the
model learns less than the count of hidden positions suggests.

The two known corrections attack that granularity. **Whole-word masking** hides all the pieces of one
word at once, so no fragment is left as a cue. **SpanBERT** goes further and hides contiguous spans of
several words, forcing reliance on broad context rather than immediate neighbours.

The general lesson outlives the case: in designing a self-supervised objective it is not enough to
fix how much signal is removed, one has to check that what was removed cannot be deduced by a
shortcut. It is the same error, at another scale, that the lesson on the encoder identified in next
sentence prediction.

## The two shapes of fine-tuning

Once pre-training is over, adaptation takes one of two shapes, depending on which part of the output
is read.

The first reads **only** the `[CLS]` vector and produces one label for the whole input. It is the
shape of sentiment analysis on a sentence and of textual inference on a pair.

The second reads **every** position and produces one label per token. It is the shape of named entity
recognition and, with a variant, of extractive question answering, where the head predicts two
indices: the token where the answer starts and the one where it ends.

In both cases the added head is small —a linear projection, in the simplest case— and the encoder
barely moves. That is the entire appeal of the paradigm: one expensive pre-training, and from there a
cheap adaptation per task.
