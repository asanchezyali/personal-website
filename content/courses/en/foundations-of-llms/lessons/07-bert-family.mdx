The previous two lessons described BERT as a model. This one walks what came after, in three
directions that do not get in each other's way: training it better, shrinking it until it fits in
production, and extending it to many languages at once. With it the pre-training arc closes.

## Training the same thing, better

The first move after BERT was the least showy. RoBERTa did not change the architecture: it changed
the training protocol. Ten times the data —160 GB against 16—, much larger batches, longer training,
and the removal of next sentence prediction, whose uselessness was documented in the lesson on the
encoder.

The technical adjustment usually cited is **dynamic masking**. BERT masks each sequence once, during
preprocessing, and reuses that same mask across every epoch: the model sees exactly the same
corrupted sentence over and over. RoBERTa generates the mask **on each pass**, so a single sentence
yields as many distinct examples as the training has epochs.

The general observation this work leaves deserves careful statement, since it tends to be overstated.
It is not that scale makes objective design irrelevant: the lesson on the encoder showed that ELECTRA
substantially improves sample efficiency with a better idea. What RoBERTa demonstrates is more
precise: **a considerable part of what was attributed to BERT's architecture was in fact insufficient
training**. Before proposing a new mechanism, it is worth checking that the old one was trained
properly.

## What a soft distribution transfers

The opposite direction seeks to shrink the model. The most widespread technique is **distillation**:
a small model, the student, is trained to reproduce the output of a large one, the teacher.

The detail that matters is **what** the student copies. Not the correct label, which is already in
the data, but the complete distribution the teacher produces. When it assigns high probability to
*cat* and, among the rest, more to *dog* than to *house*, that ordering carries information about the
structure of the problem which a categorical label discards entirely.

The obstacle is that the information is compressed against zero. To expose it, the distribution is
softened with a **temperature** $T > 1$:

$$
p_i(T) = \frac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)}
$$

The figure quantifies the effect. At $T = 1$, every class other than the correct one sums to $7.1\%$
of the mass: their contribution to the gradient is negligible and the student learns little more than
the label. At $T = 3$ they sum to $49.5\%$, and the entropy rises from $0.33$ to $1.36$ against a
maximum of $1.61$. The relative ordering is the same in both cases; what changes is whether it weighs
enough to teach anything.

It is worth noting that DistilBERT is not trained on that alone. It uses a **triple loss** combining
the distillation term with the usual language modelling objective and a cosine distance between the
student's hidden states and the teacher's. The published result is a model $40\%$ smaller and $60\%$
faster that retains $97\%$ of the performance.

## The arithmetic of compression

The reduction techniques act on different factors, which is why they accumulate. Distillation removes
**weights**; quantisation reduces the **bits** of each one, storing eight-bit integers instead of
thirty-two-bit floating point.

Starting from the count in the lesson on the parameter budget, DistilBERT's configuration —six layers
instead of twelve, with no segment table and no pooler— gives $66.4$ million parameters against
BERT-base's $109.5$, $39\%$ fewer, matching the published $40\%$. In 32-bit floating point that is
$253$ MiB against $418$; with quantisation to eight bits, $63$ MiB. The combined factor is **6.6
times**, and neither technique prevents the other.

ALBERT attacks a third factor. Besides factorising the embedding table —covered in the lesson on the
budget— it **shares one layer's parameters across the whole depth**: the twelve layers of the base
version are the same layer applied twelve times. The resulting count is around $12$ million against
BERT-base's $109.5$. The eighteen-fold reduction usually quoted corresponds to the large version,
where $18$ million face $335$. It is worth noting that sharing weights reduces memory but **not**
compute: the twelve applications still run.

## One vocabulary for every language

The multilingual extension depends on a prior decision: what a token is. It cannot be a word, because
the number of words is unbounded and new ones keep appearing. BERT uses **subwords**, covered in the
lesson on the budget: frequent ones stay whole and rare ones fragment into known pieces.

That choice is what makes a multilingual model viable. A single shared subword vocabulary covers many
languages with no word left out, and sub-lexical pieces —endings, roots— are shared between related
languages. mBERT was trained this way over 104 languages with a single embedding table.

The interesting result is **cross-lingual transfer**: fine-tune the model on a task in one language
and it solves the same task in another it was never fine-tuned on. Since every language shares one
representation space, what is learned crosses over at no extra cost.

## Aligning two languages explicitly

XLM takes the idea a step further with **translation language modelling**. Instead of monolingual
text, **aligned** sentence pairs are fed in —the same content in two languages— and masking is
applied across the whole pair.

The consequence is the one the method is after: to reconstruct a hidden word in Spanish, the model
can consult the English side, where that information is still present. Filling the blank that way
requires putting both sequences into correspondence, so the objective forces the alignment rather
than waiting for it to emerge.

From the same shared space follows a property requiring no extra machinery: switching languages
mid-sentence is processed normally, because every token is simply an entry in the one vocabulary.

## Closing the arc

Seven lessons and a single idea. Pre-training turns unlabelled text into free supervision; the three
architectures are distinguished by which positions each position may read; and BERT is the instance
that made it evident, along with the family that scaled it, shrank it and extended it.

What follows falls outside pre-training: how these models become assistants. Instruction tuning,
alignment with human preferences, and reasoning within the context.
