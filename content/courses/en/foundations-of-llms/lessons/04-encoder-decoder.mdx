The previous two lessons described families with complementary limitations. The decoder generates but
looks only to its left; the encoder has both sides but does not generate. This lesson covers the
architecture combining the two, the objective it is pre-trained with, and closes the module with a
map of every objective seen.

## The bridge between the two halves

A common description places encoder and decoder side by side without specifying what connects them.
The connector is a third kind of attention, and without it the architecture would make no sense.

A decoder layer runs **two** consecutive attention steps. The first is masked self-attention, causal,
over the tokens generated so far: the same one from the lesson on the decoder. The second is
**cross-attention**, where the queries come from the decoder but the keys and values come from the
encoder's states.

The asymmetry the figure shows is the content of the architecture. Over the target the mask is
triangular, for the usual reason: position $t$ cannot see what it has not yet generated. Over the
source there is **no mask at all**, because the input is complete from the start and there is
nothing to leak. Every generated token consults the entire input sequence.

From this follows the answer to why the encoder is kept. Remove cross-attention and the decoder is
an ordinary language model with no access to the source. Cross-attention is what makes the output
depend on the input.

## Every problem as text to text

T5 took that structure to its conclusion. If the model turns one text sequence into another, any
task can be expressed in that format: translation, obviously, but also classification —whose label
is emitted as a word— and even regression, whose number is emitted as text.

| Input | Output |
| --- | --- |
| `translate En→De: Hello` | `Hallo!` |
| `answer: when was Einstein born?` | `March 14, 1879` |
| `score a translation: ...` | `0.81` |

The uniformity has an immediate practical consequence: one model, one loss function and one training
procedure for the whole set of tasks.

Its descendants are also worth naming, since they usually go unmentioned. The prefix stating the
task —`translate En→De:`— is an instruction written into the input itself. The distance between that
and the instruction tuning that defines today's assistants is short: T5 introduced the idea that the
task is specified in the text rather than in the architecture.

## Denoising as the objective

How to pre-train the pair on unlabelled text remains. The answer is to make it a **denoising
autoencoder**: take correct text, corrupt it, hand the corrupted version to the encoder and require
the decoder to reconstruct the original.

$$
(\hat\theta, \hat\omega) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta,\omega} \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{D}} \text{CE}\!\left(\text{Model}_{\theta,\omega}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{noise}}),\, \mathbf{x}\right)
$$

The loss is the cross-entropy of the lesson on the decoder, now applied to the whole reconstructed
sequence. The label remains free: it is the text before it was corrupted.

## The repertoire of corruptions

If the objective is to reconstruct the original, the design decision is **how to break it**. BART
tried a repertoire: masking single tokens, deleting them, replacing a whole span with one marker,
permuting sentence order and rotating the document.

The variants are not equivalent, and the difference is instructive. When masking, the model knows how
many pieces are missing, since each marker occupies a position. When **deleting**, it must also
deduce **how many** tokens were removed. When replacing a span with one marker, it must deduce the
span's **length**. Each corruption defines a different difficulty, so the corruption chosen *is* the
curriculum.

The published result is concrete: BART performed best by combining **text infilling** with
**sentence shuffling**, with gains of up to six ROUGE points on abstractive summarisation, dialogue
and question answering.

## T5's sentinels

BART reconstructs the whole sentence. T5 introduces an economy: the decoder emits **only the missing
pieces**. Each removed span is replaced in the input by a distinct **sentinel**, and the target is to
produce just the spans, each preceded by its sentinel.

The gain is in sequence length, and over a realistic input it is considerable. T5's ablations settled
on a corruption rate of $15\%$ —the same one BERT uses for masking— with spans of mean length $3$.
Over a 512-token input that corrupts some 77 tokens spread across 26 spans, so the target measures
104 tokens against the 512 of the full sentence: **80% shorter**. Since the cost of attention grows
with the square of the length, shortening the target makes each training step cheaper, and it is one
of the reasons T5 scaled cleanly across sizes.

The same authors noted that differences between objectives were limited and that performance held
steady at rates between $10$ and $25\%$. The choice of $15\%$ is reasonable, not critical.

## One stack suffices: prefix language modelling

The separation between encoder and decoder is not a necessary architectural boundary. The
introductory lesson showed three attention masks, and the third —the prefix one— reproduces the
behaviour of both halves within a **single** stack of layers: prefix tokens attend to one another in
both directions, like an encoder, and continuation tokens attend causally, like a decoder.

The shift in perspective is worth stating: what distinguishes the module's three families is not so
much the architecture as **which positions each position may read**. Encoder, decoder and prefix are
three masks over the same attention mechanism.

## The map of objectives

The module has covered every major self-supervised objective. They do not divide cleanly by
architecture —several admit more than one— so it is cleaner to organise them by what they optimise.

| Objective | Idea | Fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language modelling | predict the next token | decoder |
| Masked | predict the hidden tokens | encoder · enc-dec |
| Permuted | predict in a random order | autoregressive |
| Discriminative | classify: sentence order, replaced token | encoder |
| Denoising | reconstruct corrupted text | enc-dec |

One procedure underlies all of them: manufacture a prediction problem from unlabelled text and solve
it at scale.

## What the ablation was actually comparing

The architecture is usually defended by citing T5's ablations, and the citation is frequently passed
on in distorted form. Stating it correctly requires a precision about parameter counts.

An encoder-decoder with $L$ layers in each half has roughly **twice** the parameters of an
$L$-layer decoder. Its compute cost, however, is **the same**, because each token passes through one
of the two halves and not both. The comparison the authors ran therefore pits an encoder-decoder of
$2P$ parameters against a decoder of $P$ parameters, **at equal compute**, and under those conditions
the former with a denoising objective beat both the plain language model and the prefix one.

The difference matters. Claiming it wins "at equal parameters" would be false, and would invert the
result's meaning: what it shows is that, for a given compute budget, it is worth spending parameters
on keeping the two halves separate.

## Closing the module

Three families and one procedure. Decoders predict the next token and learn to generate. Encoders
fill in blanks and learn to represent. Encoder-decoder models corrupt and rebuild, and learn both.
All three turn raw text into free supervision, which is the entire mechanism behind a foundation
model.

That closes the module on pre-training. What follows is the transformation of these models into the
assistants that get used: instruction tuning, alignment with human preferences, and reasoning in the
context.
