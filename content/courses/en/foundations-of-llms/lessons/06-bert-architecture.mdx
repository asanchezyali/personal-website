The previous lesson described how BERT learns and how it is used, and detailed the input: the three
embedding tables whose sum produces each token's vector. What remains is the body of the model, which
is a single layer repeated $L$ times. This lesson opens it.

## The repeated layer

Each layer contains **two** sub-layers. The first is self-attention, where tokens exchange
information with one another. The second is a dense network transforming each position separately,
with no communication between them.

Both share the same envelope:

$$
\text{output} = \text{LN}\!\left(F(\text{input}) + \text{input}\right)
$$

where $F$ is the corresponding sub-layer. The two elements of that envelope do work out of all
proportion to their simplicity. The **residual connection** adds the input back to the block's
output, so information has a direct path that does not cross $F$. **Layer normalisation** then
rescales the result to keep activations in a stable range.

## Inside attention

Each token vector is projected three ways: into a **query** $\mathbf{q}$, a **key** $\mathbf{k}$ and
a **value** $\mathbf{v}$. A token's query is compared against every key, and the result of those
comparisons decides what proportion of each value it collects.

$$
\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}
$$

Read left to right: $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ scores every query-key pair, the division adjusts
the scale, softmax turns the scores into weights summing to one, and those weights average the
values.

## The division by the square root of the dimension

The factor $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ is usually presented as a technical adjustment, but it has an exact
justification worth developing, because it also explains why head size does not grow as the model
scales.

Suppose the components of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ are independent, with mean zero and variance
one. Their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ independent terms of variance one, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) = d_k
$$

The scores therefore spread in proportion to $\sqrt{d_k}$. And softmax is not scale invariant:
multiplying its inputs by a constant greater than one concentrates the mass on the maximum.

## Several heads of the same size

Attention is not run once but several times in parallel. **Multi-head attention** splits the vectors
into $n_{\text{heads}}$ subspaces of dimension $d_k = d/n_{\text{heads}}$, applies the mechanism in
each and concatenates the results. Each head thus has its own set of projections and can specialise
in a different kind of relation.

There is a regularity here that usually goes unnoticed. In BERT-base, $768/12 = 64$. In BERT-large,
$1024/16 = 64$. **Head size does not change**: scaling the model widens the dimension and adds heads,
but each head keeps its 64 dimensions. Given the above, the decision is coherent: $d_k$ fixes the
regime in which softmax operates, and altering it would change the mechanism's behaviour as well as
its capacity.

## The dense block

After attention mixes information **between** tokens, the second sub-layer transforms each token
**separately**. It is a two-layer perceptron:

$$
\text{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}_2\,\text{GELU}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1\right) + \mathbf{b}_2
$$

The inner dimension is conventionally $4d$: the vector is widened, a non-linearity is applied, and it
is compressed back. The non-linearity is GELU rather than the ReLU of the original transformer.

It is worth contrasting this block with the previous one in terms of cost, because popular accounts
grant attention a prominence the parameters do not support. With the figures from the previous
lesson, in one BERT-base layer attention contributes $2.36$ million parameters and the dense network
$4.72$ million: **two thirds of every layer sit in the dense block**, and the proportion holds
identically in the large version. The mechanism that gives the architecture its name is the small
part.

## The placement of the normalisation

One detail of the envelope remains, fixed without comment by the opening equation: the normalisation
is applied **after** the residual is added. That arrangement is called post-norm, and the
alternative —normalising before the sub-layer, leaving the sum untouched— is called pre-norm.

The difference is not cosmetic. With pre-norm there is a path running from input to output adding
contributions without crossing any normalisation; with post-norm, every layer normalises the sum and
that direct path disappears. Xiong and co-authors showed in 2020 that the second arrangement produces
large gradients near the output at initialisation, and that it therefore requires a learning-rate
**warm-up** phase, while the first behaves well from the start and allows it to be dispensed with.

BERT is post-norm, following the original transformer, and its training recipe consequently includes
that warm-up. Later models moved largely to pre-norm, for the reason the figure illustrates.

It is worth closing with what the residual connection solves, since it is the condition that makes
everything else possible. Without that alternative path, stacking twenty-four layers would attenuate
the gradient before it reached the first ones. BERT-large would not be trainable.

## The next step

That completes the model: three embedding tables, a layer repeated $L$ times, and a handful of
numbers fixing its size. The architecture contains no exotic idea; it is an ordinary transformer
encoder, and BERT's leap came from the training rather than from the layer.

The next lesson walks the family BERT started: RoBERTa and what changed on retraining it with more
data, the efficient variants obtained by distillation and quantisation, and the multilingual models.
