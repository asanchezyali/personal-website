The lesson on BERT in particular described the two shapes of fine-tuning according to which part of
the output is read. A prior and more consequential decision remains: whether the encoder **moves at
all** during adaptation. Cost, the amount of data required, and how many tasks one installation can
serve all follow from it.

## The two ways of adapting

The model for a specific task is a composition. The pre-trained encoder reads the input and a small
network added on top produces the answer:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \text{Predict}_\omega\!\left(\text{BERT}_{\hat\theta}(\mathbf{x})\right)
$$

Training that composition on labelled data admits two regimes. In the **feature-based approach** only
$\omega$ is adjusted: the encoder is frozen and acts as a fixed extractor of representations. In
**fine-tuning** both move, with $\theta$ starting from the pre-trained weights.

$$
(\tilde\omega, \tilde\theta) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\omega,\,\theta} \sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{D}} \text{Loss}\!\left(\mathbf{y}_{\omega,\theta}(\mathbf{x}),\, \mathbf{y}\right)
$$

The procedure is ordinary supervised learning. The only particular thing is the starting point: it is
not trained from scratch but from a model that already represents language, which is why the labelled
set can be small. It only has to teach the task, not the language.

## The layer worth reading

Freezing the encoder raises a question fine-tuning never poses: of the twelve layers, which one is
read? The usual answer —the last— turns out to be wrong, and BERT's authors measured it on named
entity recognition.

The published figures order the options clearly. Keeping the input embeddings gives $91.0$ F1, far
below. The **last** hidden layer gives $94.9$, and the **second-to-last** gives $95.6$: reading the
final layer is worse than reading the one before it. The reason is that the last layer is the most
specialised to the pre-training objective —reconstructing masked tokens— and therefore the least
general. The study's best option is concatenating the last four, at $96.1$, against $96.4$ for full
fine-tuning.

The final difference is $0.3$ points. Freezing the encoder costs very little performance on this
task, provided the right layer is read.

## What each regime costs

Performance is not the only comparison that matters. If one encoder is to serve several tasks, the
decisive factor is how many parameters must be **stored per task**.

Full fine-tuning trains and stores BERT-base's $109.5$ million parameters **for every task**: $418$
MiB in 32-bit floating point, multiplied by the number of tasks. With ten tasks that is over four
gigabytes of weights which are mostly the same thing.

The opposite extreme, freezing everything and training a linear head, stores $6$ KiB per task.
Between them lies a middle ground that has become standard: **parameter-efficient fine-tuning**.
Instead of moving the weights, a low-rank correction is added to them —the idea of LoRA, developed in
the lesson on rank in the linear algebra course— over the query and value projections.

At rank $8$ that is $12 \times 2 \times 2 \times 768 \times 8 = 294{,}912$ parameters, $0.27\%$ of
the model, and $1.13$ MiB per task against $418$: **369 times less**. The backbone is shared and each
task contributes a small file beside it.

## The criterion for choosing

Freezing is preferable when the labelled set is very small, because the encoder cannot drift and
overfitting is confined to the head. Also when one installation serves many tasks and the memory cost
dominates.

Fine-tuning performs better when there is enough data and the task departs from the pre-training
domain, because it lets the representations specialise. Its risk is the converse: with few examples
the model can overfit or degrade what it already knew.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning occupies the middle and is why it displaced the other two in
practice: it keeps almost all the performance of full fine-tuning at the storage cost of freezing.

## The [CLS] representation is not a sentence embedding

One warning remains, worth stating precisely, because the mistake is very common.

The lesson on BERT in particular noted that `[CLS]` summarises nothing by construction and takes on
that role because training forces it to. The practical consequence is direct: **before** fine-tuning
on a task, the `[CLS]` vector is not a useful representation of the sentence. Extracting it from a
freshly pre-trained BERT and comparing by cosine similarity gives poor results.

The concrete figure comes from Sentence-BERT. Taking the `[CLS]` output of an unfine-tuned BERT, or
averaging its token vectors, produces sentence representations **worse than averaging GloVe
vectors**, which are static and much older.

What that implies is worth stating carefully, since it is usually summarised badly. The problem is
not fixed by changing the pooling: in the Sentence-BERT study, with the model already trained by
their procedure, averaging scores $87.44$ and `[CLS]` scores $86.62$ on the semantic similarity
benchmark. That is two points apart, not the distance separating either from being usable. What
Sentence-BERT contributes is not the choice of pooling but the **training objective**: a siamese
network over sentence pairs that directly optimises the property one intends to use.

The rule that follows is general. An intermediate representation is good for what it was trained for.
`[CLS]` is trained to feed a classifier, and it does that well; it is not trained to make distances
between sentences meaningful, and it does not.
